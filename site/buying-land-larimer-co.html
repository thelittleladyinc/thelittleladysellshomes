Buying Land in Larimer County, Colorado

What to Know About Septic Rules, Zoning, Wells & Building

Thinking about land in Fort Collins, Loveland, Wellington or the foothills? Larimer County has its own rules, opportunities, and gotchas — here’s what you need to know.

Water Wells in Larimer County

Well permits are issued by the State of Colorado, but you’ll want to confirm existing permits, usage restrictions, and water testing requirements.

  • Household-use only wells are typical for properties under 35 acres
  • 35+ acres may be eligible for a domestic well (includes outdoor and livestock use)
  • Always request a copy of the well permit and flow rate report

Learn more about Colorado well permitting

Septic Transfers Are Required

Larimer County requires a Transfer of Title permit for all home sales with an onsite wastewater treatment system (septic).

  • A cleanout must be installed and accessible
  • The system must be inspected and approved before closing
  • Use a county-certified septic professional

Larimer County Septic Requirements

Zoning & Setbacks

Larimer County zoning includes categories like Open, Rural Estate, and Farming zones. Each has rules about structures, land use, and density.

  • "Open" zoning preserves space but may limit subdivision or density
  • Accessory buildings like shops and barns are allowed in many zones
  • Setbacks vary and apply to homes, fences, driveways, and outbuildings

Explore Larimer Zoning Districts

Building, ILCs & Surveys

ILCs may suffice for home purchases, but if you're fencing or building, a full survey is recommended.

  • Setback verification
  • Lot line disputes
  • Placing fences or future structures

Special Considerations

In Larimer County, watch for areas that may have additional building requirements:

  • Floodplains and FEMA zones
  • Wildfire risk zones (common in foothills)
  • Private road access or easement issues
  • Development on steep slopes

Taxes, Fire Protection & Insurance

Each rural area may fall under a different fire district and tax zone. That can affect property taxes and insurance requirements.

  • Check for defensible space or mitigation requirements
  • Some areas may require cisterns or have limited hydrant access

Larimer County septic transfer: what it means when property changes hands

Larimer County requires septic systems to be inspected and brought through a transfer process when a served property sells — the county's program requires an inspection by a qualified inspector, repairs of identified deficiencies, and a transfer acceptance document, so a failing system gets caught at the sale instead of surprising the new owner a year later. The county's health department publishes the current requirements and fees; because program details evolve, check larimer.gov or ask us to verify the current process before you set a closing timeline.

Practically, this shapes both sides of a deal: sellers of septic-served property should schedule the inspection early — a required repair found late can push a closing — and buyers should read the inspection report as due-diligence gold, since it tells you the real condition of a system that costs serious money to replace. I build the septic timeline into every rural Larimer contract I write so it never becomes the thing that delays keys.

Wells and water rights on Larimer County land

If the parcel isn't served by a water district, the well permit is the heart of your due diligence: Colorado's Division of Water Resources issues permits by type, and what kind a parcel can get — household-use-only versus domestic with irrigation and livestock — depends on its water rights situation. An existing well should come with its permit, and its type needs to match how you plan to live; my acreage water guide walks through the permit types and the questions to ask.

Zoning rounds out the picture — much of rural Larimer sits in the county's Open zone district, covered in my open zoning explainer. Zoning, water, septic, and access: get those four answered and you actually know what you're buying. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Larimer County septic transfer program?

A requirement that septic systems be inspected and pass through a county transfer process when the property sells, so deficiencies are found and fixed at the transaction. The county publishes current rules and fees — verify them when you set your contract dates, as programs change.

Who pays for the septic inspection when selling in Larimer County?

It's negotiable in the contract, though sellers commonly handle the inspection and required repairs since the obligation attaches to the sale. What matters most is scheduling it early enough that a repair doesn't push the closing.

Do I need a well permit to buy land in Larimer County?

The land carries the permit situation — what you need is to verify what permit exists or what type the parcel could get from the Colorado Division of Water Resources, and whether that matches your plans for the property. Household-only versus domestic-with-livestock is a very different rural life.

Can I build a house on land I buy in Larimer County?

Usually the question is what and where, not whether: zoning district rules, setbacks, septic feasibility, access, and (in the foothills) wildfire considerations all shape it. Those answers exist before you buy — pulling them together is exactly the due diligence I run with land buyers.
