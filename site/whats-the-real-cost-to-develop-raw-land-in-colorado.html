What’s the Real Cost to Develop Raw Land in Colorado?

By Christine Gwinnup · 2025-04-28

Buying land is just the first step. Before you can build, you’ll need to bring in utilities, infrastructure, and often make major improvements. Here’s what it really costs to develop raw land in Colorado so you can budget wisely from the beginning.

1. Water: Well or Tap Fees

  • Drilling a well: $15,000–$30,000+ depending on depth and location
  • Water tap fee (if on rural water): $20,000–$40,000 in many areas
  • Water quality testing and permitting may add more

2. Septic System Installation

  • Basic 3-bedroom system: $10,000–$18,000
  • Engineered systems (poor soil or high water table): $20,000–$35,000+
  • Transfer of title inspection (if one exists already): required in Larimer County

3. Electrical Service

  • Installation is often priced per pole or foot of trenching
  • Expect $5,000–$15,000 depending on distance from transformer
  • Consider solar as an alternative if infrastructure is far away

4. Driveway & Road Access

  • Basic gravel drive (100–300 ft): $6,000–$15,000
  • County standards may apply if it connects to public roads
  • Private road agreements may be needed in rural subdivisions

5. Land Clearing & Grading

  • Tree removal and brush clearing: $2,000–$8,000+
  • Grading and site prep: $5,000–$20,000 depending on terrain
  • Don’t forget erosion control or fire mitigation if required

6. Permits and Surveys

  • Building permits: $2,000–$5,000+ based on county and project size
  • ILC or full boundary survey: $600–$2,500
  • Well and septic permits are handled separately
Tip: Budget at least $50,000–$100,000 for full development of a rural lot—not including the house. Costs vary widely based on location and infrastructure.

How much does it cost to get water to a property?

Water is usually the most consequential development question in Colorado, and it's priced two completely different ways. If a water district serves your road, you'll pay a tap fee set by that district — each district publishes its own fee schedule, they differ enormously across Northern Colorado, and they change, so the district's current schedule is the only number that counts. If no district line reaches you, you're drilling a well: pricing is per-foot plus pump and equipment, so depth to water drives everything, and only local well drillers who know the area's geology can quote it honestly.

Before any of that: confirm the parcel's water rights and what kind of well permit Colorado's Division of Water Resources would issue for it. In some areas household-use-only permits are the norm, which affects what the land can be — a homesite, versus a place you can also water livestock. My acreage guide on wells, water, and septic goes deeper on this.

How much does it cost to get electricity to raw land?

Power is priced by distance. The serving utility (Xcel, Poudre Valley REA, or another co-op depending on where the parcel sits) quotes a line extension based on how far your build site is from their existing line, whether the run is overhead or underground, and the terrain in between. A parcel adjacent to a line might connect for surprisingly little; a beautiful site a long way up a canyon can carry a line-extension quote that changes the whole project's math.

The utility will give you a real estimate for a specific parcel — often free or for a modest engineering fee — and getting that quote BEFORE you buy is one of the smartest phone calls in land buying. Off-grid solar has also become a legitimate alternative worth pricing against a long line extension.

Septic, driveway, and the costs people forget

Septic starts with a soils/percolation evaluation; the result determines whether you can use a conventional system or need an engineered one, which costs significantly more. The county health department permits the system, and on resale Larimer County requires septic inspection and transfer paperwork — covered in my Larimer land guide.

Then the quiet line items: driveway construction (length, grade, and drainage decide the price), a county access permit, site grading, and — in much of the Front Range foothills — wildfire-mitigation and defensible-space considerations that can affect insurance. None of these is a reason not to build; every one is a reason to price the specific parcel before you fall in love with it. Send me the listing and I'll help you build the real number.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to build on unimproved land in Colorado?

The land-development portion — water, power, septic, access — is priced parcel by parcel: tap fees or well depth, distance to power lines, soil results, and driveway length each carry their own quote. Budget by collecting those four quotes for the specific parcel; any single all-in number you read online is a guess.

How much does it cost to get water and electricity on land?

Water: either your water district's current tap fee, or a well quoted per-foot by local drillers. Electricity: the utility's line-extension quote, driven by distance to their nearest line. Both quotes are parcel-specific and obtainable before you buy — get them during due diligence, not after closing.

Is it cheaper to buy raw land and build in Colorado?

Sometimes — but only when the development costs are known before you buy. Cheap land far from utilities often costs more all-in than dearer land next to them. The comparison only works with real quotes on both sides.

What is a perc test and why does it matter?

A soils evaluation that determines how the ground absorbs water — it decides whether standard septic works, an engineered system is required, or the site can't support septic at all. It's one of the first things to establish on any parcel without sewer service.

Can I put a well and septic on any Colorado land?

No — well permits depend on the parcel's water rights situation and state rules, and septic depends on soils and county health requirements. Both are checkable before purchase, and both belong in your contract contingencies.
